That morning we woke up in Cappadocia at dawn. Hot air balloons rising in silence. It was the farthest point we had reached from Barcelona on that trip. From there, we would slowly make our way back home through Bulgaria and Transylvania. But not before passing by the stunning Turkish Riviera. Long roads ahead. A simple plan.

The Defender was running well. We were towing a trailer kindly lent to us by a Turkish company, Crawler. The road that day was easy. We stopped for lunch in Sarıoğlan, at a small restaurant near the gas station at the main intersection. The usual ritual: kebab, tea, and the famous Turkish ice cream show, where the vendor keeps "giving" you the cone but never actually lets you take it. Sophie was laughing. Everything felt light.

At 3pm we got back on the road.

Two minutes later, a violent metallic noise exploded from under the car.

![The Defender on the road in Turkey](/images/uploads/girl-wearing-patagonia-cap-in-a-car.jpg)

I pulled over. The Defender wouldn't move. I engaged low range. It moved again, slowly, but something was very wrong. I drove back to the gas station. No one spoke English, Spanish, Italian, or Catalan. The language barrier was total. We could not read tone or intention.

Then there was this man.

He was sitting near the pump, smoking. Calm. Watching. The younger men around him seemed to follow his lead. He spoke fluent English, with a heavy Turkish accent. His name was Asim.

He said there was only one mechanic in town. He called him. The mechanic came, barely looked under the car, said it was electrical. It wasn't. I could feel it was mechanical. But when he told me to disengage low range, the car moved again.

That felt like hope.

We tried to pay. Asim told him something in Turkish. The mechanic refused our money.

It was already 5pm. The Riviera was too far now. We chose a mountain destination less than two hours away and drove off slowly.

For a few minutes, it seemed possible.

Then the noise came back. This time louder. Metal grinding in a way that tells you something important is breaking.

> "You are a family. I cannot leave you alone. Come have dinner and stay at my mother's farm."

I texted Asim. He told us to go to the mechanic's shop. The mechanic was closing for the weekend. Asim made him stay.

This time the verdict was clear. It was most likely that the transfer case was damaged. The Defender was not going to the mountains. Not to the Riviera. Not to Dracula's castle in Romania. Not home.

The nearest proper 4x4 workshop was about 100 kilometers away, in Konya, one of Turkey's largest cities.

It was 7pm. Night was around the corner.

Asim had mentioned earlier that the nearby mountains were his land. We could drive there slowly and stay the night, and figure out Konya and the mechanic tomorrow. We did not know what to do. We had no real options.

Off we went, little by little, toward his land in the mountains. Then a message arrived. "I'm behind you. Stop at the market on your left. I have an idea," he said.

We all got out of the vehicles, and he said, "You are a family. I cannot leave you alone. Come have dinner and stay at my mother's farm. It's just five kilometers down this dirt road."

Mariella and I looked at each other.

![Family travel moment](/images/uploads/mother-and-daugther-seeing-each-other.jpg)

"My son Mete, 20 years old, will go in the car with you and guide you." They spoke Turkish to each other. We understood nothing.

"I'll get food," Asim said.

Turkey is not familiar territory for us. Different language. Different codes. Different gestures. When people hear about our travels, the overlanding, the Defender, they often ask how we are not afraid traveling like this. They imagine worst case scenarios. Thieves. Kidnappings. Traps.

Things that go wrong in foreign places. In that moment, those thoughts try to appear.

Mariella and I tried to speak to each other with our eyes. Fear is loud when you are far from home. Sophie, ten years old at the time, was relaxed. For her, it felt no different than when we once lost our dog in the mountains between Albania and Kosovo, or when we left a campsite quickly because something didn't feel right. Adventure had already taught her one rule: trust your instincts, but don't panic.

Then Mariella asked something simple. "What is your mother's favorite food?"

It was not small talk. It was a way to read the situation. If this was a story invented on the spot, the answer would hesitate. It would feel weak.

Asim answered immediately. "Meat and pasta." Clear. Natural.

Mariella smiled. "You know I am Italian. Get tomatoes and cheese. We have garlic and pasta."

He said his mother had the best tomatoes in her garden.

That was the turning point.

That night we cooked together. His 83 year old mother, wearing her headscarf, welcomed us. His son. His son's girlfriend. A cousin. The table filled with fusion Italian and Turkish dishes and laughter.

There was no trap. No danger. Just generosity.

> A few weeks ago, almost two years later, a message arrived on my WhatsApp. A picture of his cigarettes and that lighter. "Thinking of you. I hope the family is well."

They helped us organize everything. Translation. The workshop in Konya. The logistics. The repair would take a week.

We moved to a caravan lot in Konya with the trailer. We met travelers from everywhere. We went to Asim's mansion in Konya, met the rest of the family. Sophie played with his granddaughters.

The Defender was in surgery, but we were safe.

During that week my 48th birthday arrived. I told Mariella I wanted to invite Asim and his family to a nice restaurant we had found.

He refused immediately. "It is your birthday. You are my guest," he said.

He took us somewhere better. His treat.

What do you give a man like that. A strong, respected man who needs nothing from you?

He smoked a lot. So we bought him a Zippo lighter and engraved his name on it. He loved it.

A few weeks ago, almost two years later, a message arrived on my WhatsApp. A picture of his cigarettes and that lighter.

"Thinking of you. I hope the family is well."

![The Defender at sunset](/images/uploads/kings-charles-spaniel-argentina.jpg)

On the road, problems will come. Plans will break. You will not always understand the language around you. But if you stay calm, trust your team, and lean into uncertainty instead of running from it, something else appears.

Connection. Resilience. Growth.

For us, it is not about the vehicle. It is about the mindset. Stepping into the unknown with responsibility, courage, and trust in the people beside you. Leaving a better world behind us.

That is how I face challenges.

That is the Defender Family life. That is the Defender Trophy spirit.
