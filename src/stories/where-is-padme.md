We were leaving Krumë, in northern Albania, after a rough night.

The day before, somewhere near Çalaj, in the Has region, close to the Drin River and the Kosovo border, we had to get out of a situation that felt wrong. Not exactly dangerous in a clear way, but wrong enough. The kind of wrong you don’t explain perfectly later, but you understand completely in the moment.

So we left late in the afternoon and ended up sleeping outside a gas station near Krumë.

But that is another story.

That morning, the plan was simple: take a beautiful remote road south and, somehow, make our way toward Lake Ohrid to rest for a few days. After the tension of the previous night, the idea of water, calm, and doing nothing sounded like paradise.

![Aerial shot land rover defender overlander in albania](/images/uploads/aerial-shot-overlanders-albania-04.jpg)

We were not alone. We were traveling with our new German friends, Martina and Peter, the Polarvux, a retired couple who were almost full-time travelers. They were in a massive Iveco overlander with their dog Lupo. The kind of vehicle that makes our Defender look like the young cousin trying to prove he belongs at the grown-up table.

![three type of overlander vehicles](/images/uploads/three-type-of-overlander-vehicles-01.jpg)

The convoy also included Jürgen and Jutta, starting a sabbatical-year world tour in their Ford overlander, which was also big. Not Iveco big, but big enough that our Defender was clearly the small one in the group.

![Overlanders in Albania checking the map](/images/uploads/the-defender-family-with-friends-albania.jpg)

The road was every overlander’s dream.

![overlander vehicles in Albania](/images/uploads/albanian-raod-03.jpg)

Long, raw, gravel, wild. Mountains around us, concrete military bunkers scattered like ghosts from another time, and nobody in sight except the occasional local farmer. Albania is stunning, but unfortunately trash is a real problem even in remote places, so we stopped for a quick land clean-up and left that little stretch of road slightly better than we found it.

![overlanders picking up trash in Albania](/images/uploads/trash-picking-team-02.jpg)
![overlanders picking up trash in Albania](/images/uploads/trash-picking-team-01.jpg)
![girl making a heart sign with her hands standing in a military bunker in albania](/images/uploads/sophie-concrete-miltary-bunker.jpg)

We were close enough to Kosovo that at one point my phone received a message from the carrier:

> “Welcome to Kosovo”

We had not crossed a border, at least not officially, but that’s how close we were. Out there, lines on a map feel less like politics and more like a suggestion.

The road was also perfect for a drone shot.

Of course it was.

I called the others on the radio and asked them to stop while I set it up. Then we started moving again, the drone above us, the vehicles raising dust, the mountains opening around the road. From above, it must have looked incredible. Three overland trucks crossing a forgotten corner of Albania like some perfectly planned expedition.

Which, of course, it was not.

![Aerial shot land rover defender overlander in albania](/images/uploads/aerial-shot-overlanders-albania-01.jpg)
![Aerial shot land rover defender overlander in albania](/images/uploads/aerial-shot-overlanders-albania-02.jpg)

After a while, it was time to land the drone. I told the others to go ahead and that we would catch up a few miles later for coffee and a quick stretch. It was one road. No intersections. No way to get lost.

That is usually the exact sentence you say before things start going wrong.

Instead of landing the drone on the ground like a normal person, I decided to catch it with my hand.

The drone disagreed.

The propellers cut two of my fingers. Not a little scratch. A proper cut. Blood everywhere. In seconds we went from “cinematic overlanding content” to “where is the first aid kit and why is this happening?”

Mariella wanted to help, but emergencies are not exactly her fastest operating mode. She froze for a second, not knowing where to start, while I tried to do part of the job with one good hand and two bleeding fingers. We got stressed. We argued a little. You know, like perfect couples do in the middle of nowhere when there is blood, a drone, and a first aid kit involved.

Sophie looked at everything, tried to help, and then stayed quiet, reading the room in that mature way kids sometimes do when they understand things are serious. Padme, our Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, was somewhere around the car, probably wondering why humans complicate everything so much.

We opened the first aid kit and immediately discovered that it was more decorative than medical. Old band-aids, random things, nothing serious enough for a drone attack in the Albanian mountains.

So we texted Martina.

By then, she and Peter were around twenty minutes ahead of us. She replied with the kind of calm confidence only a German overlander with medical experience can have.

She had exactly what we needed.

Of course she did.

Somewhere ahead of us, in the middle of nowhere, there was basically a German mobile hospital inside a giant Iveco. With Lupo as emotional support.

We wrapped my fingers as best we could, got back in the Defender, and drove to meet them.

Twenty minutes later, Martina took one look at my hand and went to work. Alcohol. Proper cleaning. Serious bandages. Special German finger dressings that looked like they had been designed by NASA for mountain trauma situations.

It was like going to the ER, except the emergency room was a gravel road near the Kosovo border, the ambulance was an Iveco overlander, and the doctor had a dog.

![Family travel moment](/images/uploads/three-type-of-overlander-vehicles-02.jpg)

The whole thing took a while. Cleaning, wrapping, reorganizing, breathing again. The girls calmed down. I tried to recover my dignity, with limited success. The convoy waited patiently.

Then it was time to continue.

And someone said it.

> “Where’s Padme?”

At first, it felt impossible.

Padme is small. She disappears under jackets, blankets, bags, sweaters, all the soft chaos that travels with us inside the Defender. Many times we think she is missing, and then her little Cavalier face appears from under something, as if offended by our lack of faith.

So we searched.

Under everything. Behind everything. Around everything.

Nothing. Padme was not there.

In one second, my fingers stopped mattering.

The drone, the blood, the bandages, the coffee break, the road to Lake Ohrid — all of it disappeared. There was only one thought: we had left her behind.

Most likely, she had jumped out during the confusion when I cut my fingers. And nobody noticed. Which meant she could be several kilometers back, alone on a remote gravel road in northern Albania.

Mariella and Sophie were in tears.

I wanted to panic too. Padme is not just a dog. She is family. She is part of the Defender. Part of our home on wheels.

But something switched in my head.

Years ago, in Los Angeles, during my Dive Master course, I learned that in a rescue situation panic is useless, but adrenaline can be helpful if you give it a job. You need a plan. You need roles. You need everyone focused.

So I went into rescue mode

![overlander vehicles in Albania](/images/uploads/farmer-in-albanian-raod.jpg)

Martina and Peter stayed where we were, in case Padme was somehow nearby. We turned the Defender around.

Inside the car, I gave instructions.

“Sophie, you look right and back. Only right and back. Mariella, you look upper right and front. I’ll cover left and front.”

One job each.

Windows open. Calling her name. Eyes scanning the road, the rocks, the ditches, the hills.

“Padme, Padme!” Again and again.

A beat-up car passed by. We stopped and tried to ask the driver if he had seen a small dog. The scene was strange from both sides: for them, a black Land Rover Defender full of stressed foreigners in the middle of nowhere; for us, an old car appearing on an empty Albanian gravel road while our dog was missing. We didn’t speak Albanian. He didn’t speak our languages. So we did the only thing we could do.

We showed him a picture.

He looked at the phone, understood enough, but hadn’t seen her.

We kept going, Nothing.

Every curve felt too long. Every empty stretch of road felt like bad news. The girls kept calling through the open windows, their voices breaking. I kept driving and scanning, trying not to let the fear take over.

What if she had gone into the mountains?

What if someone picked her up?

What if she was hurt?

What if we didn’t find her?

Then, near the place where I had cut my fingers, we saw a kid.

He appeared almost from nowhere, walking with a bigger dog. Beautiful green eyes. Calm. Silent. In that moment, he didn’t feel like a kid. He felt like someone placed there exactly for us.

We stopped.

Again, no shared language. Just gestures, fear, and Padme’s picture on the phone.

He looked at the image.

Then he smiled, and pointed to a small hill.

There she was, Padme, our little Paddy!

Standing there as if she had simply decided to do a short solo expedition through Albania and was now ready to rejoin the family.

We jumped out of the car.

She saw us and ran.

I don’t think I had ever seen her run that fast. Ears flying, little body full speed, dust under her paws, straight toward us.

Sophie broke completely. Mariella too. I probably did as well, but for storytelling purposes I will continue to say I was still in rescue mode.

We hugged her, kissed her, checked her, cried, laughed, breathed again.

Then we turned around to thank the kid.

He was gone, just gone.

We wanted to give him something. I had a spare adventure flashlight in the car, the kind of little gift that would have been perfect for a boy walking through the mountains with his dog. But he had disappeared.

No goodbye. No wave. No chance to thank him.

Like angels do. He wasn’t scared of us. He did his mission, and then he was gone.

![overlander vehicles in Albania](/images/uploads/man-standing-with-dog-in-overlander.jpg)
![overlander vehicles in Albania](/images/uploads/the-defender-family-in-albania.jpg)

We got back to the Defender, and Padme was the first one inside. No hesitation. Straight in. From that day on, every time we stop somewhere remote and then open the door again, she jumps in before anyone else, as if to say: not making that mistake twice.

Mariella still says she was traumatized by the whole episode.

Padme, I mean.

Probably all of us.

We drove back to the convoy emotionally destroyed, with Padme safe, my fingers wrapped in German medical technology, and our dream coffee break now carrying the emotional weight of a rescue operation.

That day, Albania gave us everything in a few hours: beauty, fear, blood, chaos, comedy, and a small miracle with green eyes on a gravel road.

Adventure is never just the drone shot.

It is the mistake before it, the fear after it, the people who hold the line when things go wrong, and the little heartbeat that comes running back to you from a hill in the middle of nowhere.

And yes, I learned my lesson.

No more risky drone landings.

At least that’s what I said that day.

Now time to relax in Ohrid Lake
